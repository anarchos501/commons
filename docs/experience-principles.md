# Commons Experience Principles

Commons is mutual aid coordination infrastructure. Its interfaces should help people cooperate, organize, and care for one another without reproducing unnecessary surveillance, bureaucracy, coercion, or hidden hierarchy.

These principles guide how Commons feels to use, not only how it functions technically.

The Commons interface should feel calm, understandable, dignified, flexible, privacy-respecting, socially legible, emotionally safe, mobile-friendly, and low-pressure.

## Calm Coordination

Commons should optimize for coordination clarity rather than engagement intensity.

Commons must not use addictive interaction patterns, outrage optimization, manipulative urgency, infinite engagement loops, guilt-driven notification systems, contribution competition, top-helper dynamics, or social pressure mechanics designed to increase retention.

The goal is not maximum activity. The goal is sustainable cooperation.

The interface should help users understand what needs attention, what they can meaningfully contribute to, what they can safely ignore, what is visible to others, and how their data is being used.

Users should feel invited into coordination, not trapped inside it.

## Progressive Complexity

Commons should reveal complexity gradually.

New users should encounter simple language, minimal interface density, and only the tools necessary for immediate participation.

As participation deepens, additional coordination tools, governance tools, project management tools, moderation tools, federation tools, and advanced preferences may become available progressively.

Users must retain the ability to manually expand or simplify their interface. Advanced functionality should never be permanently hidden, and low-complexity interaction should remain valid and respected.

The interface should adapt to participation without forcing institutional fluency immediately.

## Human Coordination Over Algorithmic Authority

Commons should support human judgment rather than replace it.

The platform may assist with routing, filtering, visibility, matching, trust workflows, and notifications.

Commons should avoid opaque scoring systems, hidden ranking algorithms, behavioral optimization systems, automated moral judgment, and engagement-driven prioritization.

People should be able to understand why they received a notification, why a request was routed, why a proposal became visible, why trust was granted or denied, and why a moderation action occurred.

Coordination should remain legible and explainable.

## Dignified Request Flows

Requesting help should not feel transactional, performative, or shame-inducing.

Commons should avoid public vulnerability feeds, visible unmet-request counts, competitive scarcity dynamics, social exposure of hardship, and interfaces that frame users primarily as recipients.

Support requests should use calm and respectful language, minimize unnecessary disclosure, preserve requester privacy, allow deletion and expiration, and distinguish between coordination visibility and public visibility.

The system should remember contribution without permanently archiving vulnerability.

## Visibility And Interface Control

Users should retain meaningful control over what appears in their interface.

Commons should support visibility preferences, notification preferences, collapsed or expanded interaction surfaces, selective participation, low-noise modes, and accessibility-oriented simplification.

Examples include showing only requests matching selected service capabilities, hiding governance discussions, collapsing contribution feeds, reducing notification frequency, prioritizing local projects, and minimizing social visibility.

Users should be able to shape the amount of coordination complexity they encounter.

Future availability controls should remain private, low-pressure, reversible, and non-public. Preferences such as unavailable, available, limited, and time-sensitive capable should help contributors set boundaries and help occasionally without implying duty, reliability scoring, moral expectation, or an on-call emergency responder identity.

## Expandable Entity Surfaces

Every major entity in Commons should expose its meaningful interactions through expandable contextual surfaces, including projects, groups, proposals, service capabilities, support requests, and trust workflows.

Entity cards should begin simple, expose context progressively, support expansion and retraction, and avoid overwhelming interface density.

A user should be able to understand what something is, what actions are available, and what visibility or privacy applies without navigating deep interface hierarchies whenever possible.

## Contextual Trust, Not Social Credit

Commons should avoid global reputation systems.

Trust should remain local, contextual, scoped, explainable, revocable, and auditable.

Trust should never become permanent social ranking, algorithmic status, universal moral scoring, or contribution gamification.

Acceptable trust includes being trusted for rides within a specific group, trusted for childcare for a limited duration, or trusted for medical accompaniment after group approval.

Unacceptable trust includes universal trust scores, permanent contributor ranks, engagement-based status tiers, and hidden behavioral scoring.

Trust should communicate responsibility and accountability, not prestige.

## Emotional Safety

Commons should recognize that many users may interact with the system under stress, while exhausted, while vulnerable, from unstable environments, through low-quality devices or networks, or with limited technical literacy.

The interface should therefore prioritize calm language, low cognitive load, understandable actions, reversible interactions, visible privacy boundaries, gentle onboarding, clear consent, and predictable behavior.

Complexity should never become a gatekeeping mechanism.

## Mobile-First Mutual Aid

Commons should assume many users primarily access the platform through phones.

Interfaces should prioritize fast loading, low bandwidth usage, touch accessibility, offline resilience where possible, limited navigation depth, readable layouts, and simple interaction patterns.

Mutual aid coordination often happens during work, during emergencies, while traveling, and in unstable environments. The interface should respect this reality.

## Multilingual And Cross-Cultural Accessibility

Commons should be designed for multilingual communities.

The platform should support localization, translation infrastructure, culturally adaptable wording, language preferences, future right-to-left support, and plain-language communication.

No single language or cultural assumption should define participation legitimacy.

## Notifications Should Respect Human Limits

Notifications should support coordination without creating guilt pressure or emotional overload.

Commons should avoid urgency inflation, aggressive re-engagement, manipulative reminders, and shame-based inactivity prompts.

Users offering support remain humans with limited energy, attention, and capacity.

The notification system should help communities coordinate sustainably rather than maximize responsiveness metrics.

## Moderation And Governance Ergonomics

Commons should acknowledge that communities still require boundary setting, conflict handling, moderation, trust revocation, and accountability processes.

Moderation powers should remain visible, authority should remain scoped, actions should remain reviewable, governance should remain understandable, and moderation should not become hidden institutional control.

Administrative convenience must not override constitutional privacy protections.

## Privacy-Visible Interaction Design

Users should be able to understand who can see something, why they can see it, where data is stored, whether something syncs, whether something federates, and how long something persists.

Privacy should not be hidden behind obscure settings or legal abstractions.

The interface should make visibility and retention understandable at the moment of interaction whenever possible.

## Low-Tech Accessibility

Commons should remain usable by people with low technical literacy, older devices, unstable internet, limited time, limited energy, and limited institutional familiarity.

A user should not need to understand federation, cryptography, governance theory, replication policies, or synchronization models in order to request help or contribute meaningfully.

Infrastructure complexity should remain mostly invisible.

## Interfaces Should Expose Coordination, Not Perform Identity

Commons should avoid encouraging performative identity construction.

People are not metrics, brands, or engagement objects.

The interface should emphasize relationships, projects, responsibilities, contributions, requests, and coordination rather than status performance, follower dynamics, popularity competition, and social visibility accumulation.

Commons exists to support collective coordination, not social platform identity economies.

## Reversibility And Consent

Whenever possible, actions should be reversible, participation should remain voluntary, visibility should remain adjustable, sync should remain understandable, and governance should remain revisable.

Commons should support peaceful exit, adaptation, and institutional experimentation.

The interface should reinforce that communities are living social systems, not rigid machines.
